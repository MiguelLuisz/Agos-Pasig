About the project

Why we made this site

Agos means “flow” in Filipino. The river’s future depends on what flows into it from homes, factories, and streets.

The 27-kilometer Pasig River sits at the heart of Manila’s history. Decades of urbanization polluted it, but it’s slowly recovering. Agos Pasig tells its story and shows how communities can help.

We believe nature and urban progress can coexist, and the Pasig River can prove it.

    What is the Pasig River?

    The river links Laguna de Bay to Manila Bay. Its location makes it important at both a national and regional level.

    Length

    About 27 km

    From Laguna de Bay, through the Napindan Channel, to Manila Bay.

    History

    How the river declined, and began to recover

    1930s

    Fish migration from Laguna de Bay begins to decline.

    1940s–50s

    After World War II, population growth and industry pollute the banks. People stop bathing in the river.

    1960s

    People stop washing clothes in the river. Ferry transport declines.

    1970s

    The water turns dark and gives off a strong odor.

    1980s

    Fishing activities stop.

    1990

    The river is declared biologically dead.

    2018

    The Pasig River wins the Asia Riverprize for its rehabilitation work.

    2025

    Phase 3 of the rehabilitation project opens at Plaza Mexico, Intramuros.

    Chapter 2 of 4

    What pollutes the river, and what does that harm?

    Most pollution starts on land. It then harms wildlife, nearby communities, and Manila Bay.

    Causes

    Where the pollution comes from

    Four main sources of pollution.

    01  Household waste

    The biggest source: about 65% of waste dumped in the river comes from riverside households (ADB, 2009).

    • Many riverside homes lack sewer connections or septic tanks
    • Wastewater carries human waste, detergents, and food residue
    • Uncollected garbage piles up in drains

    Source: ADB (2009) · Visit source ↗

    02  Industrial waste

    Some nearby factories discharge wastewater without proper treatment.

    • Sources include tanneries, food processing, and chemical or metal plants
    • Wastewater may carry heavy metals and chemicals
    • Gaps in enforcement let some discharges continue

    Source: ADB (2009) · Visit source ↗

    03  Plastic and solid waste

    Limited collection and recycling let rubbish reach the river.

    • Rain washes litter from streets and drains into the water
    • Plastic breaks into microplastics that are hard to remove
    • Microplastics were found at every sampling site in a 2025 study

    Source: Ilao et al. (2025) · Visit source ↗

    04  Polluted tributaries

    Esteros (small creeks and canals) and rivers from San Juan, Quezon City, and Marikina feed the Pasig.

    • They carry sewage and rubbish from upstream communities
    • The Coast Guard calls tributaries the main contributor (2025)
    • Cleaning the Pasig alone cannot work while they stay polluted

    Source: Philstar (2025) · Visit source ↗

    Effects

    What the pollution harms

    Figures are shown only when a source supports them.

    Aquatic life

    Pollution-tolerant species dominate

    Higher oxygen demand from decaying waste leaves less oxygen for fish. Introduced janitor fish now dominate, while native species are under threat.

    Source: DENR-NCR (2025)
    Visit source ↗

    People

    Fecal coliform: 138,500 MPN/100 mL (2025)

    Fecal contamination is still high, so direct contact with the water is unsafe. Debris also blocks waterways and raises flood risk.

    Source: DENR-NCR (2025)
    Visit source ↗

    Livelihoods

    Declared biologically dead by 1990

    A river that can no longer support life can no longer support fishing, bathing, washing, or ferry travel the way it once did.

    Source: Philippine Daily Inquirer (2021)
    Visit source ↗

    Manila Bay and the ocean

    Ranked the world’s top river source of ocean plastic in a 2021 study

    Pollution flows downstream into Manila Bay. DENR questioned the 2021 ranking, so treat it as an estimate.

    Chapter 4 of 4

    Which solution can last?

    Four programs are already working on the Pasig River. We compared them and found one that prevents pollution instead of just cleaning it up.

    Existing solutions

    Four programs already at work

    A quick look at each program. Open “Details” for how it works, or “Assessment” for how it rates.

    1

    River Cleanup and Rehabilitation

    “Pasig Bigyang Buhay Muli” (PBBM)

    Led by DENR-NCR and the PRCMO

    Cleans up waste and tracks water quality, but can’t stop new pollution from flowing in.

    Details
    Current status
    The Pasig River rehabilitation has grown into a large, multi-sector program anchored by the ongoing “Pasig Bigyang Buhay Muli” urban development initiative.
    How it works
    DENR-NCR runs river cleanups and water-quality monitoring to remove built-up waste and track the river’s condition. Communities and several agencies take part, but lasting recovery also requires stopping untreated wastewater and garbage from entering the river.
    Results so far
    A technology-driven, multi-agency effort led by DENR-NCR and the PRCMO. It focuses on advanced waste interception and AI technology, links the river to infrastructure and tourism, and sets long-term pollution prevention measures.
    Assessment
    Feasible
    DENR-NCR, the PRCMO, and other agencies can coordinate cleanup, monitoring, and rehabilitation work.
    Accurate
    Water-quality monitoring helps assess the river’s condition and track environmental changes.
    Comprehensible
    Cleanups and water-quality testing are easy-to-understand ways to address visible waste and pollution.
    Inspiring
    It encourages government agencies and communities to work together toward a cleaner Pasig River.
    Robust
    It combines cleanup, technology, and infrastructure, but long-term success needs wastewater control, steady funding, and consistent enforcement.

    Sources: PCO (n.d.) ↗ · IWA (n.d.) ↗

    2

    Wastewater Treatment and Environmental Enforcement

    Manila Water, Maynilad, PRCMO, and the EPR Act

    Most sustainable

    41 plants · 410+ million liters a day

    Treats household and industrial sewage before it reaches the river, backed by monitoring and penalties.

    Details
    Current status
    Manila Water runs 41 wastewater treatment plants with a combined capacity of over 410 million liters per day, and Maynilad has invested over ₱46 billion in sewerage improvements. Untreated household sewage is still a major source of pollution because many homes have no sewer connection, and heavy rain carries wastewater and garbage into waterways. DENR is also partnering with The Ocean Cleanup on automated trash interceptors, and the EPR Act pushes companies to manage their plastic packaging waste.
    How it works
    The PRCMO tests water quality and checks that industries comply with wastewater rules. Manila Water and Maynilad keep expanding their treatment facilities so household and industrial sewage is treated before it reaches waterways.
    Results so far
    Millions of liters of wastewater are treated every day, which reduces pollution in the Pasig River. Homes without sewer connections still release untreated sewage that keeps bacteria levels high, and heavy rain still washes waste into the river. Trash interceptors and the EPR Law help keep garbage from clogging treatment facilities.
    Assessment
    Feasible
    Water concessionaires already run treatment plants, although expanding sewer connections takes significant investment.
    Accurate
    Treating wastewater before it is released directly targets sewage pollution, and water-quality testing tracks pollution levels.
    Comprehensible
    It is easy to understand: dirty water is treated before it enters rivers and other waterways.
    Inspiring
    Better sanitation and cleaner waterways can encourage communities and industries to support pollution prevention.
    Robust
    It tackles a major source of pollution, but it is limited by homes without sewer connections and untreated wastewater during heavy rain.

    Sources: Inquirer (2026) ↗ · Inquirer (2026) ↗

    3

    Solid Waste Management and Plastic Waste Reduction

    Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) Act of 2022

    155 show-cause letters issued (Aug 2026)

    Makes companies recover and recycle their plastic packaging so it stays out of waterways.

    Details
    Current status
    The EPR Act of 2022 requires covered companies to recover and manage their plastic packaging waste through recycling and recovery programs. In August 2026, the government issued 155 show-cause letters to companies that may be violating EPR requirements. Stronger enforcement and proper waste management are still needed to keep plastic out of rivers.
    How it works
    Covered companies must collect, recycle, and recover their plastic packaging waste. LGUs and communities help through waste segregation, proper disposal, and organized collection, while environmental agencies monitor compliance, especially to keep waste out of waterways during heavy rain and flooding.
    Results so far
    The program pushes companies to collect, reuse, and recycle plastic packaging, helping reduce pollution in the Pasig River and prevent clogged drainage. The 155 show-cause letters show that compliance is being monitored, but real impact still has to be measured through verified recovery and recycling data.
    Assessment
    Feasible
    Businesses can work with recyclers, waste collectors, local governments, and communities to recover plastic waste.
    Accurate
    Recovery reports and recycling data can measure progress, but 155 show-cause letters alone do not prove river pollution has gone down.
    Comprehensible
    It is clear: companies must take responsibility for the plastic packaging their products create.
    Inspiring
    It encourages businesses and communities to reduce waste, recycle, and dispose of trash responsibly.
    Robust
    It can cut the plastic entering waterways, but long-term results depend on enforcement, verified recovery, and public participation.

    Sources: RA 11898 (2022) ↗ · PCO (2026) ↗

    4

    Manila Bay Clean-Up, Rehabilitation, and Preservation Program

    Supreme Court continuing mandamus

    178 LGUs · only 15% sewer coverage in NCR

    A Supreme Court–ordered program coordinating agencies and LGUs across the whole Manila Bay watershed.

    Details
    Current status
    Still ongoing since the Supreme Court’s 2008 order requiring 13 government agencies to rehabilitate Manila Bay and its connected rivers, including the Pasig–Marikina–San Juan river system. In December 2025, DILG-NCR held a stakeholders’ forum to review progress, identify challenges, and improve coordination among LGUs.
    How it works
    It runs in three phases: (1) cleaning waterways, reducing pollution, repairing sewer lines, and improving waste management; (2) rehabilitating sewer systems, expanding wastewater treatment, and addressing informal settlements along waterways; and (3) public education, law enforcement, and water-quality monitoring. Illegal structures along river easements are also removed, with LGUs doing the work under DENR and DILG monitoring.
    Results so far
    The program covers 178 LGUs across NCR, Region III, and Region IV-A, and 135 of them (76%) have approved 10-year solid waste management plans. But only about 15% of NCR’s water-served population is connected to sewerage, around 233,000 informal settler families live along waterways, and the Pasig River is still unsafe for swimming.
    Assessment
    Feasible
    Possible through cooperation among agencies and 178 LGUs, but sewerage expansion and relocating informal settler families take a lot of time and resources.
    Accurate
    Water-quality monitoring and tracking waste management plans help measure progress, though the data show major pollution problems remain.
    Comprehensible
    It uses clear approaches: waterway cleanup, sewerage improvements, waste management, and public education.
    Inspiring
    The Supreme Court order and agency cooperation encourage continued action toward cleaner waterways and Manila Bay.
    Robust
    It tackles several connected sources of pollution, but lasting results need long-term funding, enforcement, wider sewer coverage, and public participation.

    Sources: DENR (n.d.) ↗ · DILG-NCR (2025) ↗

    Comparative analysis

    How the four solutions compare

    Each one helps, but each has a limit. The question is which limit is easiest to overcome.

    Existing solutionWhat it doesMain limitation
    1River Cleanup and RehabilitationRemoves waste, monitors water quality, and restores the river through government and community efforts.Cannot prevent pollution if garbage and untreated wastewater keep entering the river.
    2Wastewater Treatment and Environmental EnforcementMost sustainableTreats wastewater and monitors compliance to reduce harmful discharges into waterways.Needs significant funding, maintenance, and wider sewerage coverage.
    3Solid Waste Management and Plastic Waste ReductionPromotes plastic recovery, recycling, and proper waste disposal through the EPR Act.Depends on public participation, accessible collection services, and consistent enforcement.
    4Manila Bay Cleanup and Rehabilitation ProgramCoordinates agencies and LGUs to reduce pollution across the watershed and connected rivers.Faces challenges in coordination, wastewater treatment, and waste management infrastructure.

    Why Solution 2 is sustainable

    Stop the pollution before it reaches the river

    Treating wastewater before it enters the Pasig River prevents pollution for the long term.

    • Prevents pollution at its sourceLess harmful waste enters the river.
    • Provides continuous protectionTreatment plants run every day.
    • Ensures accountabilityMonitoring and penalties keep industries in line.
    • Protects health and ecosystemsCleaner water means fewer health risks for people and wildlife.
    • Builds on existing systemsIt grows from the plants and sewers already in place.

    Making Solution 2 work

    Fixing the river takes two levels of action

    Government and water companies

    Treat it and enforce it

    1. Connect more homes to sewersOnly about 15% of NCR’s water-served population is connected to a sewer.
    2. Expand treatment plantsBuild and upgrade plants to clean more wastewater.
    3. Enforce discharge rulesTest factory wastewater and penalize illegal dumping.
    4. Keep trash out of the systemStop garbage before it clogs drains and treatment plants.
    5. Monitor and publishTest water quality regularly and share the results publicly.

    Not just a choice, it’s an order: Since 2008, a Supreme Court order has required agencies to clean up Manila Bay and its rivers, including the Pasig.

    You

    What you can do

    1. Bottle your cooking oilPour cooled oil into a bottle and trash it, not the sink.
    2. Save water at homeShorter showers and fixed leaks mean less wastewater.
    3. Keep drains clearNever throw trash into canals or storm drains.
    4. Report illegal dischargesTell your barangay or the DENR about wastewater dumping.
    5. Learn and shareShare sourced facts, like the ones on this site.

    Not near the Pasig River? These habits still help. Every drain leads to a river or the sea, so cleaner water at home means cleaner waterways wherever you live.

      Our comments, conclusions, and recommendations

      What we think after our research

      These are our own views, based on what we learned while building this site.

      Comments

      Nature

      We depend on nature every day, but we often forget its resources aren’t unlimited.

      Habits

      Pollution isn’t caused by one person. It builds up from repeated habits in homes, businesses, and communities.

      Basic services

      People can only do their part if the basics work: garbage collection, sanitation, and wastewater treatment.

      Information

      The facts about the river exist, but they’re buried in reports most people never open. Clear information is part of the fix.

      Conclusion

      Our conclusion

      Cleanups treat the symptom. Stopping what flows in treats the cause.

      River recovery takes more than removing visible garbage. Some water-quality indicators improved in 2025, but others got worse, so the job isn’t done (DENR-NCR, 2025).

      Lasting change means keeping sewage, industrial wastewater, and solid waste out of the river and its tributaries, and changing the habits that send them there.

      “Nature can recover without us. We can’t survive without nature.”

      Recommendations

      Government

      • Prioritize wastewater treatment and affordable sewer connections.
      • Publish water-quality results in plain language, not only technical reports.
      • Enforce laws against dumping and illegal discharges consistently.

      LGUs and barangays

      • Strengthen garbage collection and segregation.
      • Set up drop-off points for used cooking oil and e-waste.
      • Clean and monitor the esteros, not just the main river.

      Businesses

      • Treat their own wastewater before it leaves their sites.
      • Recover the plastic packaging they produce.
      • Be open about their waste and compliance.

      Schools and communities

      • Teach the causes and effects of water pollution.
      • Organize cleanups and native planting with proper safety guidance.
      • Use online tools to report pollution and share verified facts.
